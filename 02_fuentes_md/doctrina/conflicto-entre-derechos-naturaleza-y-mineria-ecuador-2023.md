Abstract
1. Global emergence of Rights of Nature (RoN) has gained momentum since Ecuador
became the first country to constitutionally recognize it in 2008. The shift from
perceiving nature as an object, to granting it legal subjecthood, can revolutionize
protection of ecological systems. In 2021, Ecuador's Constitutional Court issued
a landmark ruling, halting mining in the Los Cedros Protected Forest.
2. Three pillars form the basis for legal protection of Los Cedros: (i) the right to
```
timely, Free Prior Informed Environmental Consultation, (ii) application of the
```
```
Precautionary Principle in risk to RoN, and (iii) the Right to Water.
```
3. We analyse the Court ruling to identify legal frameworks applied then map and
rank mining risk to other protected forests, Indigenous territories, unprotected
native ecosystems, biodiversity and areas of water resource conflict to determine
potential scale of conflict between mining and RoN.
4. 7813 mining concessions of 22,812km2 overlay 9.2% of Ecuadorian mainland,
```
2323 concessions (29.7%) overlap 16,081km2 of protected forest (4781 km2 ,
```
```
20%), Indigenous territory (6473 km2, 8%) and native vegetation outside pro-
```
```
tected areas and Indigenous territories (13,390 km2, 9%). With 80% of their
```
protected forests at risk from large-scale mining, the most impacted Indigenous
communities are the Shuar.
5. Synthesis and applications: The Los Cedros legal case in Ecuador sets a precedent
for using RoN to challenge mining in 4781 km2 of similar Protected Forest, with
potential to protect an additional 16,081 km2 of Indigenous lands and biologically
important ecosystems. However, lack of biological data for these areas will neces-
sitate extensive data collection, possibly through community-empowering citizen
science. Our study emphasizes the urgent need to integrate indigenous and tra-
```
ditional ecological knowledge (ITEK), law and ecology. We propose a new trans-
```
disciplinary field of ‘ecological forensics’ to support nature protection within the
RoN framework. Our research also identifies areas where RoN could effectively

Social and cultural consequences are often associated with the
marginalization of local populations, weak political institutions and
```
corruption (Kuecker, 2007; Vela-Almeida & Torres, 2021). Typically,
```
social conflicts escalate due to infringement of collective and indi-
vidual rights of local communities and unequal distribution of profits
```
derived from mining enterprises (Velásquez, 2012). The institutional
```
fragility of Ecuador's government poses challenges to the effective
implementation of free prior and informed consultation and public
```
engagement in extractive projects (Vela-Almeida & Torres, 2021)
```
leading to enduring social disputes stemming from violations of col-
lective human and environmental rights. Frequently, residents face
criminalization when they protest against issues such as transbound-
ary water concerns, contamination resulting from mining, deforesta-
tion and inadequate access to water resources and entitlements
```
(EJAtlas, 2020; Kuecker, 2007). While legal systems primarily based
```
on concepts of private property and underground resource rights
tend to favour extractive industries, a significant paradigm shift is
underway with the emergence of Rights of Nature laws.
```
The Rights of Nature (RoN) movement, as succinctly summa -
```
```
rized by Chapron et al. (2019), signifies a profound shift in the way
```
humans perceive and interact with the natural world. It recognizes
the shortcomings of existing environmental laws in addressing the
```
degradation and destruction of nature (Chapron et al., 2019), thus
```
inspiring the emergence of legal initiatives that acknowledge the
rights or personhood of nature and ecosystems. The recent global
```
surge in RoN is well- documented (Putzer et al., 2022) through the
```
```
Ecojurisprudence Monitor (www.ecojurispr udence.org), signifying
```
a transition from perceiving nature as an object exploited by hu-
mans to a more balanced and harmonious relationship. This shift
challenges the prevalent anthropocentric worldview that places
humans above nature by promoting a perspective where humans
are an integral part of nature. The goal is to foster a vision of mu-
tual respect, tolerance and harmony among all beings, humans
```
and nature alike, to ensure long-term coexistence (Guayasamin
```
```
et al., 2021). Ecuador became the first nation to include Rights
```
```
of Nature (RoN) in its constitution in 2008, specifically through
```
Articles 71–74, which recognizes Nature as a subject under law
with rights and not as an object susceptible to appropriation. It em-
powers civil society to represent nature as a subject and protects
ecosystems against degradation and loss. The implementation of

mining in other protected forests and Indigenous territories, and ex-

where Nature could be impacted.
Since the favourable judgement in the Los Cedros case in 2021, no
studies have been conducted to determine the scale of potential future
legal conflicts between mining activities and biodiversity conservation,
as well as impact on water resources. Therefore, the primary objective
of this paper is to gain insight into the crucial factors that influenced
the Constitutional Court's decision in favour of the LCPF and assess
the wider implications in addition to identifying regions in continen-
tal Ecuador that might be involved in future legal disputes under the
```
Rights of Nature (RoN) framework. To achieve this, we
```
1. Analyse the LCPF case from the perspective of the initial
plaintiffs and the final court decision to identify key factors
```
that are important in identifying future cases;
```
2. Identify protected forests, native forests and Indigenous territo-
```
ries that could potentially be affected by mining activities;
```
3. map species diversity, species rarity (assuming a correlation with
```
endemism) and threatened species to identify regions with high
```
```
biodiversity; and
```
4. Identify areas with potential conflicts associated with mining con-
cessions or mining activity, including those associated with con-
flict over water resources.
Our discussion and conclusion identify knowledge gaps and rec-
ommendations for addressing future RoN cases within Ecuador.

2.1 | Study area and background
```
Ecuador is a country of 248,196 km2 (excluding the Galapagos
```
```
Islands) located in North-West South America (Figure 2).
```
Administratively, it is divided into 24 provinces and 221 cantons.
```
Within these, Decentralized Autonomous Governments (GADs) are
```
charged with developing and executing Development and Territorial
```
Plans (PDOTs). These plans aim to organize and manage the physical
```
environment, local populations, socio-economic activity, territorial
```
systems, land use and their interrelationships (Peñaherrera, 2016).
```
Geographically, continental Ecuador has three important ecogeo-
graphical regions: the Coast, the Andes and the Amazon, representing
a wide variety of climates. The Ministry of Water, Environment and
```
Ecological Transition (MAATE) has established 30 protected areas
```
```
(Coast = 9; Andes = 11; Amazon = 10) classified within the categories
```
```
of the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) under
```
```
Ecuador's National System of Protected Areas (SNAP). Additionally,
```
```
there are 173 protected forests (PFs), a category outside SNAP and
```
IUCN. Within SNAP anthropogenic activities are regulated or even
forbidden, but in PF, higher levels of human activity, such as mining,
are often allowed but depend on the forest management plan. In
```
addition, Municipal Protection Systems (ACUS) serve to protect im-
```
portant areas, created, managed and controlled by GADs. It should
be noted that central government is the only power that can define
whether mining can take place in any of these areas. Between 2018
and 2021, the Ecuadorian government established mining conces-
sions, open to bids, that overlap ACUS, Indigenous territories, PF and
```
unprotected native vegetated ecosystems (Figure 2).
```

2.2 | Analysis of the Constitutional Court ruling
in the Los Cedros legal case
To illustrate the guiding principles applied in the case, and future
implications in the conservation of ecosystems threatened by min-
```
ing, we analysed the Constitutional Protection Action (‘Accion de
```
```
Proteccion’ No. 10332-2018- 00640) which was filed as an appeal to
```
protect constitutional rights in 2018, and was selected for the final
```
ruling by the Constitutional Court in 2021 (No. 1149-19-JP/21).
```
We compare the legal rationale of the Protective Action with the
final Constitutional Court ruling to identify the scope of future, sim-
ilar cases, and emerging legal frameworks important in addressing
potential conflict between RoN and mining. We used the ‘qualita-
```
tive document analysis’ approach (Cane & Kritzer, 2012) to extract
```
information from court documents and categorize them to identify
patterns. In this qualitative legal analysis, we extracted all laws
used in the two main court documents, the Protection Action and
Constitutional Court ruling, and categorized them within their main
```
legal categories (i.e. Environmental Law, Human Rights law, Civil
```
```
Rights law and Rights of Nature). Applying the Kelsen Pyramid of
```
```
laws (Olechowski, 2018), we classified laws within a hierarchy, from
```
the ‘grundnorm’ as the foundational norm to the top-norm. Using
this classification we filter and identify laws used in both the appeal
and the ruling, those presented in the appeal but not used by the
Court in its ruling, and those used by the Court alone that were ab-
sent in the appeal. The analysis allows us to assess the implications
of the final ruling.

3.1 | Legal deconstruction of the Los Cedros rights
of nature case
The Constitutional Protection Action filed focused its principal legal
argument on three main branches of Law: Human Rights, Environmental
Law and the Rights of Nature enshrined in Constitutional Law
```
(Figure 3). The laws represented all levels of the Kelsen pyramid of
```
```
laws: Constitutional Law and International Law,2 National Legislation,
```
and environmental and forestry codes. The Protective Constitutional
Action drafted by the lawyers of Los Cedros used all normative orders
to strengthen their legal points. For example, the right to environmen-
```
tal consultation is cited in the Constitution (Art. 398). The core of its
```
legal argument relied on the standards established by the Constitution
for environmental consultation. The Constitutional Court, on the other
hand, based its ruling on just two of the three branches of law used in
the Protection Action appeal. The legal argument of the ruling focuses
```
on three main elements (Figure 4).
```
The implications of the Los Cedros ruling for the conservation of
Ecuadorian ecosystems, based on our legal analysis, identify three
core pillars of the ruling.
• Right to timely, free, prior and informed environmental consultation
```
(FPIEC)—the Court ruled that FPIEC applies to all impacted areas
```
and must be carried out, at least, prior to the Environmental
Registration phase of mining activities. The community must be
understood in the broadest sense, and standards for environ-
mental consultation are the same standards as for free, prior and
informed consultation of Indigenous peoples, except for any dif-
ferences defined by the constitution itself.
• Precautionary principle—the precautionary principle must be
applied where there is potential risk of species extinctions, de-
struction of ecosystems, permanent alternation of natural cycles
```
(Rights of Nature, Constitution Article 71), threats to maintenance
```
and regeneration of life cycles, structure, function and evolution-
```
ary processes (Rights of Nature, Constitution Article 73).
```

The Los Cedros case clearly set a global legal precedent in defend-


processes and lack of environmental consultation. This case settles ju-

4.4 | Free, prior and informed environmental
consultation
Indigenous territories have proven to be highly effective in miti-
gating deforestation and are recognized as crucial areas for bio-
logical conservation, carbon sequestration and climate change
```
mitigation (Simkins et al., 2023; Walker et al., 2014). These terri-
```
tories have maintained remarkable levels of biodiversity where
Indigenous lifestyles align more harmoniously with natural ecologi-
```
cal processes (Garí, 1999; Garnett et al., 2018; Sze et al., 2022). In
```
Ecuador, Indigenous territories encompass a significant portion of
the country's land, with 33% of the territory legally recognized as
```
Indigenous land, surpassing the proportion found in Brazil (13.5%;
```
```
Begotti & Peres, 2020). Our research underscores the importance
```
of Indigenous territories in the preservation of biodiversity, as we
have observed significant overlap between biodiversity measures
and Indigenous lands. Indigenous territories support more than
1184 species, of which more than 90 are considered internation-
ally threatened by the IUCN. Furthermore, the extensive pres -
ence of mining concessions highlights the imminent threat to
these territories. Ecuadorian law does mandate the requirement
of FPIEC for further exploration and extraction, which can serve
as a powerful tool in mitigating mining risks within these territo-
ries. However, to be truly effective, this process must be conducted
independently, as mining interests often employ extensive invest-
ment in project ‘socialization’ that can influence local populations
to support mining while concealing actual long-term impacts and
```
costs (Avcı & Fernández-Salvador, 2016). The Ecuadorian constitu-
```
tion of both 1998 and 2008 recognizes the right of all people to
be consulted when activities are planned with significant potential
```
environmental impacts. The latest constitution (article 398) shows
```
interesting advances in this aspect, with many similarities between
Indigenous consultation and environmental consultation, making
both consultations similar. The Los Cedros ruling expands the scope
of FPIEC standards for Indigenous territories to encompass all eco-
```
systems including protected (PF), unprotected ecosystems and
```
non-Indigenous land. This suggests that implementing legal strate-
gies to ensure effective an independent FPIEC has the potential to
contribute to the protection of approximately 16,080 km2 of native
vegetated ecosystems identified in our study.
4.5 | Water issues
Impacts to water quantity and quality from mining activity are
predicted for a number of cities in the south of Ecuador including
Cuenca, Yanantza and Zamora, and for catchments of the Shuar
territory. Mining exploration and extraction activities pose various
risks including water contamination during exploration and extrac-
tion, reduced water supply and the undermining of authority of
```
community-managed water boards (Velásquez, 2012). Allocating
```
water usage to extractive industries without adequate information
on availability can lead to conflicts over water rights for drinking
water and agriculture. Even when water resources are sufficient,
the inequitable distribution resulting from poor maintenance
and weak institutional infrastructure can escalate social tensions
```
(Molden, 2019). Aquatic systems face enduring risks even after
```
mining activities end. Learning from the disasters of the Mariana
and Brumadinho tailings dams in Brazil in 2015 and 2019, which
resulted in 281 deaths, is essential to address these ongoing chal -
lenges. The mine waste tailings, stored behind the failed dam from
Mariana's Samarco mine, reached the mouth of the Doce River,
spanning 663 km and is still causing health and livelihood impacts.
In Brumadinho, the collapse of the Vale dam contaminated 250 km
of the river, severely affecting Indigenous, quilombola and fisher
```
communities (de Freitas et al., 2019). Ecuador, planning large- scale
```
mining over 12,333 km2 , will require similar tailings dams for waste
storage within river basins. However, Ecuador's high seismic activ-
ity, especially in areas such as the Kutucú and Condor Cordilleras
```
known for events of magnitude up to Mw 7.0 (Parra et al., 2016),
```
heightens the risk of similar disasters. This situation is particularly
concerning given the concentration of proposed mining conces-
sions in these seismically active areas.
The initial screening of risk to water systems, by integrating
catchment modelling and utilizing database information on mineral
composition within each mining concession, could provide valuable
insight into project risk. A potential solution lies in implementing
```
participatory water monitoring mechanisms (Himley, 2014), the in-
```
formation generated can aid in the development of effective legal
strategies and the design of early warning and monitoring pro-
```
grammes (Rivera-Parra et al., 2021) while empowering communities
```
by providing them with a better understanding of risks and greater
```
control over local water resources (Scott-Bottoms & Roe, 2020).
```
However, achieving success in these endeavours requires the
strengthening of local community-based institutions and a more de-
centralized distribution of power in decision-making processes. This

emergence of the RoN.
5 | CO N C LUS I O N S
The landmark Los Cedros case in Ecuador has set a crucial legal
```
precedent in the domain of Rights of Nature (RoN) and provides
```
a framework that can be utilized in similar cases involving mining
threats. This framework has the potential to safeguard 16,080 km2
of PFs, Indigenous and non-Indigenous territories, and native vege-
tated ecosystems. Our study has identified locations within Ecuador
where future RoN cases may emerge, underscoring the importance
of biological and ecological knowledge in formulating strong argu-
ments for ecosystem protection. To maximize the success of legal
challenges against current or planned mining exploration and ex-
traction activities, we suggest focusing on areas characterized by
high species richness and the presence of threatened and endemic/
rare species. By combining this ecological emphasis with approaches
that prioritize the protection of FPIEC and safeguarding water re-
sources, the chances of achieving positive outcomes can be signifi-
cantly increased.
To strengthen the scientific framework of RoN and broaden its
scope beyond the threat of species extinction, it is imperative to
enhance our understanding of the potential impacts on ecosystem
structure, function and evolutionary processes. This effort entails
conducting comprehensive data collection on various aspects, in-
cluding species richness, threatened species and endemic species
in areas that may become the subjects of legal cases. Citizen and
civic science approaches show great potential in filling knowledge
gaps but require adequate funding investment and capacity-building
initiatives. Furthermore, the establishment of a transdisciplinary
collaboration between ITEK, ecology and law, facilitated through a
newly emerging field known as ‘Ecological Forensics’, is essential.
This field will ensure that legal systems have access to well-informed
western and Indigenous scientific information when dealing with
RoN cases, thereby bridging the gap between ecological under-
standing and the decision-making processes of the legal system. By
integrating this knowledge into legal considerations, the RoN frame-
work can be strengthened and expanded to encompass a broader
range of ecosystem-related concerns. It is clear that the overlap
of mining concessions with protected areas, native vegetated eco-
systems and Indigenous territories leads to social conflicts that not
only pose challenges to mining operations and state infrastructure
but also impact the overall security of the state itself. In order to
prevent protracted conflicts and promote sustainable outcomes,
our ultimate recommendation is to reassess mining concessions in
the following areas: protected forests, Indigenous territories, native
ecosystems with high biodiversity value and locations susceptible
to conflicts related to water resources. This recommendation aligns
